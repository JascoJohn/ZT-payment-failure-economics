## Executive summary

<p class="lede">The Economics of Payment Failure &middot; ZT East Africa &middot; January 2022 – April 2024</p>

<p class="opening">The {{failed_fees_m}} in fees sitting on failed transactions is not one problem. It is two, and they need different responses.</p>

#### 1. Extreme account behaviour: {{anom_m}} ({{anom_share}}) <span class="pill teal">Contain</span>

A small number of accounts produced sudden, massive bursts of failed attempts, most of all in December 2023 and January 2024. Twenty extreme customer-months and one steadily escalating account (47087) drove most of the jump in the headline. The pattern looks like automated retries or card testing, although the data cannot confirm that. These accounts inflate the total. They need detecting and containing, not a product overhaul.

#### 2. Remaining exposure: {{rm_m}} ({{rm_share}}) <span class="pill amber">Improve</span>

This is everything left once the extreme-volume anomalies and the escalation account's other observations are set aside. It covers every channel, but card carries {{rm_card_share}} of it ({{rm_card_m}}) and USD card alone {{rm_usdcard_share}} ({{rm_usdcard_m}}). The picture is steady and stubborn: between {{rem_range_words}} of payment attempts failed in every one of the 28 months. USD card payments fail {{usd_card_range}} of the time across the five markets, while mobile money fails {{mm_mk_range}} of the time in the same markets. Even among the {{gap_n}} customers who use both, card fails about {{gap_gs0}} percentage points more often. The channel analysis therefore points to USD card completion as the main improvement opportunity.

#### Where the money sits

Card carries {{card_share}} of all fees on failed transactions. Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda card hold most of what could realistically be recovered. Bank transfer works well, failing only {{bank_rate}} of the time, but it is too small to make a difference.

#### What recovery is worth

If USD card failure came down to the mobile money level in each market, the platform would have earned about {{rc_base_k0}} more in fees on successful payments over the 28 months, roughly {{rc_base_ann0}} a year. The range is {{rc_con_k0}} (conservative) to {{rc_opt_k0}} (optimistic). This is before processing costs, it assumes the gap is actually closed, and it is not a forecast. Against a mobile money benchmark that leaves out the escalation account, the base case would be {{bx_inc0}}.

#### Three actions

1. **Contain the anomalies.** Set up two account alerts, one for sudden spikes and one for steady escalation, so that accounts generating thousands of failures are throttled and reviewed in the same month.
2. **Improve card completion in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda.** These three markets hold {{rc_pri_k0}} of the {{rc_base_k0}} base case. Start with the merchant-months that had no successful card payments at all, and look at acceptance, routing and currency set-up.
3. **Report three numbers every month:** the count failure rate, the failed-fee share, and fees on successful payments at constant 2022 exchange rates. Track every intervention against its own baseline.

#### Bottom line

A large part of the {{failed_fees_m}} comes from a small number of accounts with extreme failure volumes. The rest is a steady, long-running problem in which USD card is the main part that can be improved. Closing even part of the gap between card and mobile money is worth real money, and most of that opportunity sits in three markets.
